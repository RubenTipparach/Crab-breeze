City benchmark, RTX 3070

Crab Breeze on the owner's desktop, the night of 27 to 28 October 2026. Standing in the capital in the cockpit, the median frame fell from - to -, five to nine times faster, and still short of 60 fps (16.7 ms). What holds it back now is video memory: the game no longer fits on the card beside the desktop's other programs.

What the GPU was drawing

Standing in the capital on the old build, one part switched off at a time. GPU time a frame, its shadow pass darker. Single runs of this scene vary up to two times, so only the big drops count: buildings and the sun's shadows.

Shadow passEverything else

Buildings within 250 m were drawn piece by piece (window frames, ledges, parapets, roof props) and every piece cast shadows into three cascades. Detailed dressing now stops at 150 m. Grass casts shadows only within 25 m, window glass casts none, and cars draw their small parts only near.

The memory spill

The RTX 3070 has 8 GB of video memory. Before the game starts, the desktop's other programs (Chrome, Firefox, Discord, Steam, Parsec, BlueStacks) already hold about 2.5 GB of it. The game then needs about 5.9 GB. Only about 5 GB fits, so Windows puts the rest in ordinary system memory, which the graphics card reaches over the PCIe bus at a small fraction of its own memory's speed. Every pass that touches those buffers then waits, and the card shows 100 % busy while doing far less work.

How much spills changes from minute to minute with what the other programs hold, and with memory the last game run has not yet given back. That is why the same scene took the GPU a few milliseconds in one run and over a hundred in the next, with the same triangles drawn.

Where the images go

The probe's census in the capital: video memory of the loaded images, by what they are.

The eight cockpit maps at 4096 px are a quarter of all image memory. At 2048 px each takes a quarter of the space: 128 MB instead of 512 MB. On the owner's direction every 4096 px texture goes to 2048 px.

Flying flat out across the map

About 33 km in ten minutes at 60 to 165 m/s. Median frame of each run, in flight. Old against new differs by two fixes: trees judged by their true distance (a forest flown over at 1 km was drawn in full and cast shadows) and the Mac session's traffic step cut. The runs overlap, so no speedup is claimed.

Bringing down twenty houses

Debris limits cut to 200 moving and 800 resting bodies within 250 m (was 600, 2 500 and 400 m), and the sun's storm shade rebuilt off the main thread (it took about 100 ms every 16 s). The worst frames fell; the medians moved with the memory spill.

At night

The suburbs held still from the cockpit (suburb-stand), the clock held at noon or midnight, each suspect switched off alone, three rounds interleaved. Shadows are the one steady cost: with them off, midnight ran fastest in every round, faster than noon too. Midnight against noon on its own moved with the round, so it is not a result. The street lamps' real lights are not the cost.

The moon's own shadows

The moon now casts in 2 cascades out to 250 m instead of the sun's 3 out to 700 m. Old build against new at midnight in the suburbs, each on its own data, one game at a time: as fast or faster and fewer frames over 16.7 ms in every round, but the median gain is about the spread between rounds, so it is a small gain, not a proven one.

Next

  1. Done: every texture capped at 2048 px, against the video-memory spill above.
  2. Warm up the shaders the loading screen misses: 35 compiled in the first second of a run (a 2.1 s stall) and more at the first shots.
  3. Shadows: about 1.5 ms of the frame on the CPU at any hour in the suburbs, every house, tree and patch of grass drawn into each cascade.
  4. Then the CPU: about 15 ms a frame in the render world (6 000 draws, mesh uploads as buildings are dressed) and the cockpit's screens cube, redrawn about 60 times a second.
